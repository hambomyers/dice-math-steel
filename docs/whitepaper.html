DICE · MATH · STEEL

Satoshi’s setup, with dice — a white paper for the kitchen table

hambomyers · v0.5 · dicemathsteel.com

ABSTRACT.

Your money is guarded by a secret number. Whoever knows the number owns the coins. This paper is one story told five ways: make the number, hide the number, prove the number, rest, and spend — without ever trusting a machine's word for anything.

EXPERIMENTAL — UNREVIEWED. Do not use with real funds. Signet or pocket change only until strangers smarter than us have broken and repaired this. That is not a disclaimer; it is the development model.

0.The whole machine, on one page

1.The number itself

Before the dice, meet the thing they make. Computers store every number as a row of switches — each switch is off or on, written 0 or 1, and each switch is called a bit. Small rows make small numbers: two bits can only say four things (00, 01, 10, 11); ten bits can count to about a thousand.

Your key is a row of 256 bits. That does not sound like much until you feel what doubling does: every added bit doubles the count of possible numbers. By bit 33 you pass every human alive. By bit 90, roughly every grain of sand on Earth. By 256, the count of possible keys is comparable to the number of atoms in the visible universe — squared past, actually. A thief cannot try them all. Nobody can. That is the entire security of Bitcoin: not a lock, a haystack wider than physics.

Its proper name is the private key — just “a 256-bit number,” nothing more exotic. You may have heard of SHA-256; that is a different creature which happens to share the number 256: a mathematical blender that grinds any input into a 256-bit fingerprint. Your key is not “a SHA-256 number” — SHA-256 is one of the tools the machine uses on the road from key to address. And the fact that anchors everything: your key never appears on the blockchain. Ever. Neither does the pad. The chain only ever sees public artifacts — your address (the key’s one-way public face) and, on spend day, a signature (a proof you know the key, built to reveal nothing about it). The blockchain is a bulletin board of addresses and proofs; the secrets stay at the kitchen table.

So when this paper says “the number,” picture it plainly: a row of 256 zeros-and-ones, written in pencil on a sheet of graph paper at your kitchen table. Everything else — plates, machines, addresses — exists to guard, split, and prove that one handwritten row.

2.Make the number

A die works like a coin:

roll 1 · 2 · 3 → write 0
roll 4 · 5 · 6 → write 1

Roll 256 times: that string of 0s and 1s is your key — a number so large that every computer on Earth, guessing until the sun burns out, would never find it. You just made it with your hands. Then roll 256 more times for a second number, the pad. It is the scrambler.

DEEP DIVE · why dice — why not just flip a coin?

A coin works; the protocol only needs 256 fair yes-or-nos. But dice are the better instrument, for reasons people have actually measured. A caught coin lands same-side-up about 50.8% of the time — physicists predicted it, then a study of 350,000 real flips confirmed it: the flip remembers a whisper of how it started. (At 256 bits even that bias would leave a thief hopeless — but take free fairness where it is offered.) Casino dice, meanwhile, are the only five-dollar object on Earth machined for fairness — cut to half a thousandth of an inch, sharp-edged, translucent to expose voids, because money rides on them nightly.

One deliberate waste, confessed: a die could yield 2.58 bits per roll, and we keep only one — because “1-2-3 low, 4-5-6 high” is a glance, and base-six arithmetic is a mistake waiting for a tired hand. We spend entropy to buy simplicity. Dice are cheap; errors are not.

Why dice? Randomness is the one thing you can never check by looking at it — a rigged machine's "random" looks identical to real random. So no machine is allowed near this step. Physics rolls; you write.

DEEP DIVE · rolling five at a time (and the sorting trap)

Throw five dice at once and you harvest five bits per throw — about fifty throws and the key is done. Physics has no objection: colliding dice do not coordinate; if anything they scramble each other harder. Just give them room and violence — casinos make the dice hit the back wall for a reason — and reroll anything cocked or stacked.

The real trap is reading order. Your key is a sequence, not a pile — and if you choose the order after seeing the faces, your tidy hands will impose patterns. Sorted dice look like ten bits but carry far fewer: entropy quietly thrown away, invisibly, into a key that still looks like perfect static. The fix costs nothing: fix the order before the throw — five colors, always read red-orange-yellow-green-blue, or a tray lane read left to right as they lie. Never touch, never tidy, never sort.

DEEP DIVE · what if I misread a die?

A misread cannot weaken the key. Read a 4 as a 1 and you have simply selected a different one of the 2²⁵⁶ possible keys — every path through the haystack is equally dark. You cannot add randomness to what is already maximal, and you cannot subtract it with a slip: stirring milk that is fully mixed changes nothing. The pencil row IS the key; the dice were only its author.

The only error that matters is your paper and the machine disagreeing — a mistyped digit births an address your steel cannot open. And the ceremony already hunts exactly that, twice, before a single sat is at risk: re-derive from the stamped steel (must reproduce the stamped address), then the $5 round-trip. In this design a lie and a typo are the same event — a disagreement between two records — and everything built to catch one catches the other.

A thief holding one plate holds pure static. Not "hard to crack" — nothing there: every digit on plate B hides a perfect 50/50 coin toss, because the pad bit beneath it was a fair roll. This is the one-time pad — 1940s spy mathematics, never beaten, because there is nothing to beat.

DEEP DIVE · the static contains every key

Here is how completely one plate hides the number. Take plate B alone and ask: which keys could it conceal? Answer: all of them — every one of the 2²⁵⁶ possible keys corresponds to exactly one pad that would produce this exact plate, and every pad is equally likely, because each pad bit was a fair roll. The plate is not a puzzle with one answer; it is a sentence consistent with every answer. That is why this is not “encryption too strong to break” — there is nothing to break. A thief with one plate and infinite computers holds the same information as a thief with none: the number is somewhere in the haystack. He knew that already.

Recovery is the same game played again: line up the plates, mark same-or-different, and the key reappears. Pencil, paper, ten minutes — the entire instruction your heirs will ever need. But make two copies of each plate: losing either half forever is losing the coins.

4.Prove the number

An address is your number’s public face — mathematics turns key → address in one direction only. The whole world can pay the face; only the number behind it can spend. That math is too big for hands, so one small machine performs it. And we trust it not at all — because its homework is graded twice, by older and stranger judges than any gadget:

THE MACHINE — $4a bare little board, no radio by anatomy, firmware you compile yourself. its one job: turn your number into your address.
THE JUDGES — $5, onceBitcoin Core — Satoshi’s own software, run by anyone, checks the public math lands on the same address. Then the ledger itself: pay the address, spend it back out. Proof, end to end.

THE VERIFIER OF LAST RESORT IS BITCOIN ITSELF — NOT US

The address is stamped onto plate B as the forever answer-key: any year for the rest of your life, re-run the math, and it must land on that exact address or something is lying. A second stranger-machine still exists in this design — but it waits for spend day, where it has the one job nothing else can do yet: witnessing the signature’s secret ingredient in the minutes before the network confirms.

This is the paper’s quiet thesis: Satoshi’s cold storage was the reference client trusting its random number generator. History punished exactly that trust. Keep his verifier; replace his RNG with dice.

5.Rest

Money arrives at the address, on the public ledger — not "into" any device. Your gear sleeps in a drawer for years. Your fortune is arithmetic, resting in two pieces of steel.

7.Scenarios, at the kitchen table

A burglar steals one plate. He is holding a photograph of TV static. This is not a treasure map torn in half — torn halves leak. It is the magic-decoder trick: two sheets of random dots that show a message only when stacked. Either sheet alone is just dots. That is not a metaphor for the math; it is the math.

Your attorney holds plate A. The oldest arrangement in banking: a safe-deposit box opens with two keys turned together — yours and the banker’s. The attorney is the banker: he safeguards a provably meaningless object under professional duty, can take nothing alone, and spending now requires a visit during business hours. Robbers hate time. “Half of it is at a law office” turns a five-minute crime into a two-day conspiracy. Who holds which plate is your policy — written in steel, invisible to the chain, executable by a family with a pencil. (Digital Bitcoin does the same trick with Taproot: multi-party rules that look like one plain key. Your plates are the analog edition.)

A house burns down. Duplicates fix loss — under the spare-car-key law: nobody keeps the spare in the car. The copy of plate A must never live anywhere plate B lives, or whoever holds that place silently holds the whole key. Copies protect against fire only if they never merge the two worlds the split created.

Someone photographs a plate. Nothing was “stolen,” yet if plate A becomes effectively public, plate B stops being half a secret and simply is the secret, wearing a costume anyone can remove. Think two-keys-launch-the-missile: you may guard one launch key harder than the other — tape one to the console and you no longer have a two-key system with one weak key; you have a one-key system. The rule in full: secrecy may be tiered, never waived; and availability is absolute — losing either plate forever loses the coins.

Decades later, your heirs. Both plates on a kitchen table, a pencil, same-or-different down 256 columns, ten minutes: the key returns. No computer, no company, no permission — and no vendor that must still be in business. A hardware wallet’s inheritance plan depends on a firm surviving; this one depends on subtraction surviving.
